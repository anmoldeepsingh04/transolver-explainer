Transolver: attention over physical states, not mesh points

Wu, Luo, Wang, Wang & Long · Tsinghua University · ICML 2024 · arXiv:2402.02366 · code

A physics simulation describes a field (pressure, velocity, stress) on tens of thousands of mesh points. Transformers are a good fit for learning these fields, but standard attention compares every point with every other point. Transolver first groups the points into a few learned "slices" of similar physical state and then runs attention only between those groups.

01 · Setting the stage

The problem: learning to solve PDEs on meshes

Partial differential equations (PDEs) govern airflow over a car, stress in a material and fluid moving through rock. Numerical solvers discretise the domain into a mesh and solve on it. For complex shapes this can take hours or days per design.

Neural PDE solvers (also called neural operators) learn the mapping from inputs to the solution from data. Once trained, they predict a new solution in a fraction of a second.

Formally, a domain \(\Omega\) is discretised into \(N\) mesh points \(g \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times C_g}\), optionally with observed quantities \(u \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times C_u}\). The model must predict the target physical quantity at every mesh point.

Example: car design (Shape-Net Car)

Input: the car's mesh (32,186 points: positions, signed distance, normals). Output: surface pressure and surrounding air velocity, from which the drag coefficient is computed.

Example: airfoil design (AirfRANS)

Input: an airfoil shape, Reynolds number and angle of attack (32,000 points). Output: velocity and pressure fields, from which the lift coefficient is computed.

Two challenges the paper identifies

① Efficiency

Tens of thousands of irregularly placed points. Standard attention has quadratic cost in N and quickly runs out of GPU memory.

② Learning the right correlations

Physics involves intricate, long-range interactions. Learning them directly between massive numbers of individual points is hard. Linear-attention variants fix the cost, but their attention tends towards uniform and carries little information (see Table 5).

ViT-style patches, which group nearby pixels, don't help either: regular square patches don't fit unstructured geometry, and they group points by location rather than by physics.

03 · The core idea

Learn the physical states hidden behind the mesh

The key observation is that the mesh is only a finite sampling of an underlying continuous physical field. Many points behave similarly. On a moving car, the surface splits into a few physically consistent regions such as the front, the bevel and the back.

Transolver therefore softly assigns every mesh point to M learnable slices. Points in a similar physical state end up in the same slice. Each slice is summarised into one physics-aware token, and attention runs between these M tokens.

Slices ≠ spatial patches

Classical domain decomposition (e.g. in FEM) cuts the mesh into local regions by hand. Learned slices can join distant points. In the paper (Fig. 1e) the car's windshield, licence plate and headlights fall into the same slice because they all face the oncoming wind, which matters for drag.

Why do slices become "physical"?

Slice weights are computed from each point's features, so points with similar features get similar weights. Training then sharpens this: tokens that mix different physical states make the attention between tokens less useful, which raises the loss (Remark 3.1).

The four equations of Physics-Attention

① Slice: soft assignment (Eq. 1)

$$w_i = \mathrm{Softmax}\big(\mathrm{Project}(x_i)\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times M}$$

A point-wise linear layer maps C channels to M scores. The softmax runs over the slices, so each row sums to 1 (Σj wi,j = 1) and assignments stay low-entropy (sharp).

② Aggregate: one token per slice (Eq. 2)

$$z_j = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_{i,j}\,x_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_{i,j}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times C}$$

Each token is the weighted average of the features of the points in its slice.

③ Attend: between tokens (Eq. 3)

$$q,k,v=\mathrm{Linear}(z),\quad z'=\mathrm{Softmax}\!\Big(\tfrac{qk^\top}{\sqrt{C}}\Big)v$$

Ordinary attention, but over M physical states instead of N points, so the attention matrix is only M×M.

④ Deslice: back to points (Eq. 4)

$$x'_i = \sum_{j=1}^{M} w_{i,j}\,z'_j$$

Each point gets back a mix of the updated tokens, weighted by the same slice weights.

Total cost: \(O(NMC + M^2C)\). With M fixed and M ≪ N, this is linear in the number of mesh points. Multiple heads split the channels into subspaces, as in a standard Transformer.

06 · The full model

Transolver = a Transformer with Physics-Attention

The rest of the model is a standard pre-norm Transformer. Inputs are embedded by a linear layer, passed through L layers, and projected to the outputs. The only change is that self-attention is replaced by Physics-Attention.

$$\hat{x}^{l} = \text{Physics-Attn}\big(\mathrm{LayerNorm}(x^{l-1})\big) + x^{l-1},\qquad x^{l} = \mathrm{FeedForward}\big(\mathrm{LayerNorm}(\hat{x}^{l})\big) + \hat{x}^{l}$$

with \(x^0 = \mathrm{Linear}(\mathrm{Concat}(g,u))\) and a final linear projection producing the predicted fields (Eq. 6).

08 · Theory & evidence

    
Why should this work?

    
1 · Attention as a learnable integral operator

    

      
Neural operators update a field layer by layer with a kernel integral, so each point is influenced by every other point in the domain:

      
$$\mathcal{G}(u)(g^*) = \int_{\Omega} \kappa(g^*, \xi)\, u(\xi)\, d\xi$$

      
Earlier work (Cao 2021; Kovachki et al. 2023) showed that standard attention is a Monte-Carlo estimate of this integral, using the N mesh points as samples (Lemma A.1):

      
$$\mathcal{G}(u)(g^*) \approx \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{\exp\!\big(W_q u(g^*)\,(W_k u(g_i))^\top\big)}{\sum_{j}\exp\!\big(W_q u(g_j)\,(W_k u(g_i))^\top\big)}\, W_v u(g_i)$$

      
Theorem 3.4 says Physics-Attention also approximates this integral. It changes the variable of integration from the mesh domain Ω to a slice domain Ωs. The token values are the continuous version of Eq. 2,

      
$$u_s(\xi_s) = \frac{\int_\Omega w_{\xi,\xi_s}\,u(\xi)\,d\xi}{\int_\Omega w_{\xi,\xi_s}\,d\xi},$$

      
and attention between the M tokens approximates the integral over Ωs. So Transolver remains a valid neural operator while attending over far fewer "integration points".

      
The proof assumes a countable domain (to build a bijection between Ω and Ωs, Lemma A.3) and smooth slice weights (to make it a diffeomorphism). It shows the design is consistent with operator learning; it gives no approximation-error bounds.

    

    
2 · Attention between tokens is sharper (Table 5)

    

      

        
The paper measures how far the learned attention weights are from a uniform distribution, using KL divergence. Higher means more informative attention. Linear attention over mesh points (Galerkin Transformer) is close to uniform; attention between slice tokens is not.

        
Related evidence: replacing learned slices with fixed 4×4 regular squares worsens results on every benchmark, even on Darcy, which is a regular grid (0.0059 → 0.0088).

      

      

    

  


  
  

    
09 · Experimental setup

    
Eight benchmarks, four kinds of geometry

    
Six standard benchmarks (from FNO and geo-FNO) plus two industrial design tasks, covering solid mechanics, fluids and porous flow. The paper compares against more than 20 baselines: neural operators (FNO, U-NO, LSM…), Transformer solvers (GNOT, ONO, Galerkin…) and geometric deep models (PointNet, GraphSAGE, MeshGraphNet…).

    

    
Metric: relative L2 error \(\lVert u-\hat u\rVert / \lVert u\rVert\) (lower is better). For car and airfoil design the paper also reports the error of the drag/lift coefficient and Spearman's ρ, which measures whether the model ranks designs in the right order. ρ is what matters for shape optimisation.

13 · Wrap-up

    
Takeaways

    

      
1 · Change what attention sees
Don't attend over N mesh points. Attend over M learned physical states: slice → aggregate → attend → deslice.

      
2 · Linear and mesh-agnostic
The cost is O(NMC + M²C), and the point-wise projection works on point clouds, structured meshes and unstructured meshes.

      
3 · Strong empirical results
Best on all eight benchmarks: a 22% average gain on the standard six, and better drag and lift ranking for real design tasks.

    

    
Questions worth discussing

    
Each question points to evidence in the paper itself.

    

      
Are the slices really "physical states"?
The evidence is mainly visual (Fig. 1, Fig. 5, Appendix D) plus the intuition in Remark 3.1. Nothing in the loss requires slices to be physical; they emerge because sharp, meaningful slices lower the prediction error. How would you test this quantitatively?

      
When does the speed advantage actually appear?
The ablation's efficiency numbers use only 1,024 points. There, time per epoch barely changes from M = 1 to M = 1024 (37.8 → 40.5 s). The linear-complexity benefit matters at large N, as Table 10 and the Shape-Net Car comparison show.

      
Is M = 1 just global pooling, and is the model then just pooling?
Yes: with one slice, every point receives the same global token. That variant performs much worse (e.g. 0.0148 vs 0.0064 on Elasticity), which suggests the interaction between several physical states matters.

      
Why does a very large M hurt?
The authors suggest the physical domain becomes fragmented, giving many noisy tokens to attend over. Each point's output is also rebuilt from only M tokens per head, so M sets a limit on representational capacity in both directions.

      
How strong is the theory?
Theorem 3.4 places Physics-Attention inside the integral-operator framework, but it relies on assumptions (a countable domain, smooth slice weights) and gives no error bounds.

      
What about time-dependent dynamics?
Most benchmarks are steady-state mappings from input to field. Only Plasticity and Navier–Stokes involve time. How would Transolver behave over long autoregressive rollouts?

      
Are the comparisons fair?
Training settings are shared with the baselines and many hyperparameters were searched, but some baseline numbers are taken from earlier papers (Appendix B.3) rather than re-run.
